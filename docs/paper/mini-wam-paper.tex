\title{Building a World Action Model from Scratch:\\Design Decisions, Lessons, and a Map of Open WAMs}
\author{Keivalya Pandya\\Northeastern University}
\date{}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
A vision-language-action (VLA) policy learns what to do. A world action model (WAM) also learns what will happen when it does it. Today's WAMs are large video models that few people can train, which makes their design hard to study. We introduce \textbf{mini-WAM}, a 0.75M-parameter WAM written from scratch in about 1,000 lines of PyTorch, and use it to walk through the decisions every WAM makes: what to predict, how actions meet the future, which backbone to use, and what the prediction is for at test time. We place eight open-source WAMs on the same map. On a toy navigation world with a true simulator, mini-WAM's latent dynamics imagines 15 steps ahead with ${\sim}80\times$ less state error than persistence. Three lessons stand out. Expert-only data hides action effects: counterfactual error grows $15\times$ while factual error even improves. Prediction helps the policy only when the observation hides state: with pixel input, success rises from 36\% to 80\%, and a separate matched pilot suggests that most of such a gain comes from state supervision rather than from dynamics. Pixels dominate the cost: the image decoder holds half the parameters and $90\%$ of the training compute. Code, data and every figure's source are public.
\end{abstract}

\section{From acting to predicting}

A VLA maps an image, an instruction and the robot state to an action. Nothing in its training asks what the action will do. A world model answers exactly that question, but does not choose actions. A WAM does both with one network (Figure~\ref{fig:paradigms}). Recent surveys define WAMs as models that ``connect future-state modeling with executable action generation'' \citep{lu2026survey} and sort them by the future they produce: pixels, latents, or none at all \citep{shen2026survey}.

Open WAMs now reach billions of parameters \citep{liao2025genie,kim2026cosmospolicy,li2026lingbotva}, and Genie Envisioner pretrains on a million real robot episodes \citep{liao2025genie}. That scale buys capability, but it hides the basic questions behind compute. What does the dynamics part actually learn? When does predicting the future make the policy better? Which choices are expensive? Conceptual tutorials map this design space \citep{zhang2026tutorial}, and minimal codebases exist for video prediction \citep{huang2026nano}, but neither lets you train a complete WAM and look inside it in an afternoon.

We build that WAM. Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}\itemsep0pt
  \item \textbf{A map of WAM design decisions}, with eight open-source WAMs placed on it (Figure~\ref{fig:design}, Table~\ref{tab:wams}).
  \item \textbf{mini-WAM}, a from-scratch WAM in which each module added to the VLA is under 100 lines, plus a toy world with a true simulator for counterfactual tests (Section~\ref{sec:build}).
  \item \textbf{A record of what worked and what did not}, from data and losses to decoder design, measured over independent runs (Section~\ref{sec:lessons}, Table~\ref{tab:lessons}).
  \item \textbf{A cost breakdown} that shows where a small WAM spends parameters and compute (Section~\ref{sec:efficiency}).
\end{itemize}
An unrelated paper also uses the name MiniWAM \citep{chen2026miniwam}; our project is a separate educational implementation.

Three of these systems report lessons that our small model reproduces. GR-1 finds that predicting a few frames ahead helps more than predicting the next frame \citep{wu2023gr1}; mini-WAM shows the same horizon trade-off (Section~\ref{sec:lessons}). Cosmos Policy fine-tunes its planning model on policy rollouts, failures included, before trusting its predictions \citep{kim2026cosmospolicy}; mini-WAM's world model also needs non-expert actions. V-JEPA 2-AC plans in representation space and avoids generating pixels \citep{assran2025vjepa2}; in mini-WAM, pixels are where most of the cost goes (Section~\ref{sec:efficiency}).

\paragraph{Architecture.} Figure~\ref{fig:arch} shows the full model. We keep the encoder of a small VLA and add three modules: the latent dynamics $f_\theta$, a linear readout $R$ and an image decoder $D$. A linear readout makes the latent interpretable by construction: if position and velocity are linear functions of $z$, the rows of $R$ say which directions encode them. The image encoder ends in a spatial softmax, which turns each of 16 feature maps into an $(x, y)$ keypoint we can draw.

\paragraph{One training step.} We sample windows of $H$ steps from one episode, encode only the first observation, and roll the dynamics forward with the actions that were actually taken (Figure~\ref{fig:train}). The loss is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{act}} + w_s\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{state}} + w_i\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{image}} + w_z\,\frac1H\sum_{k=1}^{H}\big\|\hat z_{t+k}-\mathrm{sg}[\mathrm{enc}(o_{t+k})]\big\|^2 ,
\label{eq:loss}
\end{equation}
where the state and image terms compare readouts of the encoded and imagined latents with the true current and future states and frames, and $\mathrm{sg}$ stops gradients through the targets. We use $w_s=0.5$, $w_i=w_z=1$ and $H=5$. Removing every term except $\mathcal{L}_{\text{act}}$ gives the VLA baseline, with identical encoders.

\paragraph{A toy world built for checking.} We train on a 2D point mass with momentum ($v_{t+1}=0.8v_t+0.01a_t$) and two goals; the instruction names the goal (Figure~\ref{fig:toy}). The six-number state is fully known, a true simulator answers counterfactual questions, and 400 episodes collect in seconds. Velocity never appears in an image, so a single frame cannot reveal it. A PD expert provides demonstrations, and short bursts of random actions are mixed in. We tag every transition, so the policy imitates only the expert while the world model learns from everything.

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics{\figpath/fig-toy-world.pdf}
\caption{\textbf{The toy world.} (a) Frames rendered by the environment during one expert episode. A red point mass must reach the goal named in the instruction; the other square is a distractor that appears only in the image. Momentum makes velocity part of the state, but no single frame shows it. (b) Actions in one training episode. \label{fig:toy}

\paragraph{Protocol.} Each configuration runs three times, each with its own data, split and initialization. We report mean $\pm$ standard deviation across runs and show each run as a dot. Imagination is evaluated on held-out episodes: we encode one frame, roll the dynamics forward, read out the state and compare it with the truth. For \emph{factual} error we use the recorded actions. For \emph{counterfactual} error we use random action sequences and compare with the simulator.

\section{What worked, and what did not}
\label{sec:lessons}

Table~\ref{tab:lessons} summarizes every design decision we tested. The figures that follow show the most informative ones.

\paragraph{The latent world model imagines states well, pixels poorly.} Fifteen steps into an open-loop rollout, state error is $0.0071 \pm 0.0004$, about $80\times$ below persistence; under random actions the 10-step error is $0.0072$, versus $0.388$ for ignoring the action (Figure~\ref{fig:imagine}). The decoded frames tell a different story: they stay worse than simply copying the current frame at every horizon. Figure~\ref{fig:strip} shows why: the decoder draws the agent and the instructed goal but loses the distractor.

\paragraph{Expert-only data hides action effects.} Trained on expert demonstrations alone, the world model predicts recorded trajectories \emph{better} than with exploration, yet its counterfactual error is $15\times$ worse (Figure~\ref{fig:coverage}). In expert data the action follows from the state, so a network can fit the future without reading its action input, a world-model form of causal confusion \citep{dehaan2019causal}. The data still identifies the dynamics: an affine model of state and action, given the known clipping rule, fits the transition to numerical precision from deterministic expert trajectories alone. The failure therefore lies in what the neural model learns, not in what the data contains. A few random bursts fix it.

Exploration conditions also change trajectory distributions, so this is a comparison of datasets rather than a controlled causal estimate.}

\paragraph{The model extrapolates, imperfectly.} Asked to push in one direction for 15 steps, far longer than any training burst, mini-WAM moves the agent the right way but stops short (Figure~\ref{fig:whatif}). Its endpoint error is $0.103 \pm 0.010$ arena units, against $0.457$ for a prediction that ignores the push.

\paragraph{Keypoints learn to look only when they must.} With the state as input, the 16 spatial-softmax keypoints sit almost still near the image center. When the state is removed, they lock onto the agent and the goals (Figure~\ref{fig:keypoints}). An encoder learns to localize only when nothing else tells the network where things are.

\paragraph{When prediction helps the policy.} With the state as input, the WAM never meaningfully beats the VLA, and with 25 demonstrations it trails until training gets more updates (Figure~\ref{fig:policy}a). With pixels only, the WAM wins every run, $80.3\pm12.0\%$ versus $36.0\pm19.8\%$ (Figure~\ref{fig:policy}b). A matched pilot separates the ingredients: supervising the current state lifts behavioral cloning from $17.0\%$ to $28.4\%$, and adding future-state prediction reaches $33.6\%$ (Figure~\ref{fig:policy}c). Most of the gain is state supervision; predicting the future adds a smaller, less consistent benefit.

\section{Where the cost goes}
\label{sec:efficiency}

Figure~\ref{fig:efficiency} profiles the trained model. Acting costs the same for WAM and VLA, 0.42\,ms per step, because the deployed policy never calls the dynamics. Training is a different story. One WAM step costs $38\times$ a VLA step, and the image decoder accounts for most of it: removing it cuts the step from 2.55\,s to 0.27\,s and halves the parameters. Imagination itself is cheap. Ten latent steps with state readouts take 0.28\,ms; decoding a frame at every step raises that to 12.7\,ms. Three changes made mini-WAM fast enough to run 12 three-run experiments in an afternoon: predict states rather than pixels where possible, keep the dataset on the GPU and index batches instead of looping over samples (4.5\,ms to 2.6\,ms per batch even on a CPU), and roll out in latent space.

\centering
\includegraphics{\figpath/fig-efficiency.pdf}
\caption{\textbf{Pixels dominate the cost of a small WAM.} (a) Parameters per module for the VLA baseline, a WAM that predicts only states, and the full WAM; the image decoder holds half of the full model. (b) Wall time of one optimizer step at batch size 64 (log scale). Dropping the decoder makes a WAM step $9.5\times$ cheaper. (c) Latency of imagining $k$ steps from one observation, reading out states only or also decoding every frame, compared with the cost of one policy action (dashed). Medians of repeated calls on one CPU thread of an Apple-silicon laptop; the numbers compare components with each other, not hardware.}

\section{Limitations}

mini-WAM is a teaching and research instrument, not a robot controller. The toy world is fully observed when the state is given, its dynamics are affine away from the walls, and its goal coordinates appear in the state. The policy never uses imagination at test time, so none of our results measure planning. In the pixel-input setting the WAM receives privileged state targets during training, and a history-based baseline is still missing; velocity is hidden in a single frame, and a short frame history may close much of the gap. Our studies use three runs per configuration and five in the pilot, which is enough to see large effects but not small ones.

\section{Conclusion}

A WAM is a VLA with a learned simulator that shares its representation. Built from scratch at 0.75M parameters, that simulator imagines accurately, reads out linearly and responds to actions, provided the data contains actions worth responding to. Our experiments point to three practical rules. Collect diverse actions for the world model, even while the policy imitates the expert. Expect prediction to help the policy when the observation hides the state, and test whether plain state supervision explains the gain. Predict compact states or latents before pixels, which is where most of the cost lies. The large open WAMs in Table~\ref{tab:wams} make the same choices at a scale where they are expensive to test; mini-WAM makes them cheap to test.
